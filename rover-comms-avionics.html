What the subsystem has to do

The University Rover Challenge runs in open desert terrain, and the rover has to be driven entirely from a ground station that can be hundreds of meters away with no line-of-sight guarantee. That puts two hard requirements on my subsystem: the link can never be the reason we lose the rover, and every board, motor, and camera on the vehicle has to get clean power and a data path back to the onboard computer.

So the work splits into two halves that have to agree with each other — the radio link that carries video and commands, and the avionics that distribute power and data inside the chassis. I own both, which means most of my job is making sure a decision on one side doesn't quietly break the other.

The communications architecture

A dual-band link, deliberately split so that losing one band doesn't mean losing the rover.

The ground station computers sit behind a router that feeds two radios through a PoE injector: a Rocket M900 on 900 MHz and a Rocket M2 on 2.4 GHz. Each drives its own cross-sectional antenna aimed downrange. On the rover, matching omni-directional antennas feed a second pair of Rocket radios, which land on an onboard PoE switch shared by the Jetson and the Raspberry Pi.

The two bands do different jobs. 2.4 GHz carries the bandwidth — camera feeds and everything the driver needs to see. 900 MHz carries less data but propagates far better through terrain and vegetation, so it's the band that keeps commands and telemetry flowing when the high-rate link degrades. The omni-directional antennas on the rover matter for the same reason: the rover turns constantly, and it can't be responsible for keeping itself pointed at the ground station.

The avionics and power architecture

One battery, several voltage rails, and a lot of things that all want to be the priority.

Everything on the rover runs off a single 100 A battery through a central electric power system that breaks out the rails each load needs — 12 V to the motor controllers, 15 V to the Jetson, and 5 V to the servos and logic. The Jetson AGX Xavier is the main compute; a USB hub aggregates six camera feeds into it, and a pair of Arduinos handle low-level I/O with the second one carried as a backup.

Actuation is where the current goes. Four Spark Flex controllers drive the wheels, two Spark Max controllers run the REV NEO arm motors, three more flex controllers live on the arm itself, and two RoboClaw 2×15 A controllers handle the payload servos. The science payload and the LED status mast hang off the same tree.

The rule I've held to across the whole harness is that power and data never share a run: 12 AWG for power, 24/26 AWG for data, colour-coded and documented, so that when something misbehaves in the field we can trace it without pulling the rover apart.

What I learned building it

  • Redundancy has to be designed in, not added later.Splitting bands only helps if the system genuinely degrades gracefully onto the surviving one — that's an architecture decision made at the start, not a patch.
  • The documentation is part of the hardware.A wiring diagram that's a week out of date is worse than none, because people trust it. Keeping these drawings current is most of what makes the subsystem maintainable by anyone but me.
  • Field conditions are the real spec.Bench performance told us very little about what the link would do across desert terrain with the rover moving and turning.
  • Leading a subsystem is mostly interfaces.Most of my time goes to the boundaries with the mechanical, arm, and software teams, making sure what I promised them is what they actually get.
