The instrument

L-MAPS is a radiometer designed to fly on the Artemis Lunar Terrain Vehicle and measure the heat flow beneath it as the rover traverses the surface. Heat flow is one of the more direct ways to probe the Moon's interior structure and thermal history — you're measuring the energy still leaking out of a body that's been cooling for billions of years, and inferring what's underneath from the rate.

Doing that from a moving vehicle, in the lunar thermal environment, is what makes the instrument hard. It has to be sensitive enough to resolve a small signal and rugged enough to survive a launch, a landing, and the surface itself.

What I do on the project

Flight hardware moves slowly for a reason. Most of the work is making sure a decision is defensible before it's made.

RF hardware

Parts research

Working through RF component options against the instrument's requirements — what's available, what performs, and what the tradeoffs actually cost us.

Heritage

Flight heritage checks

Verifying that candidate parts have a track record in a comparable environment. Heritage is one of the strongest arguments a spaceflight part can have.

Procurement

Vendor coordination

Sourcing, lead times, documentation, and the planning that keeps a hardware build from stalling on a part nobody ordered in time.

Alongside that, I work with the team and our JPL collaborators on mission planning and documentation. It's a mix of careful record-keeping and practical engineering judgment: every component has to make sense for this instrument, in this environment, on this mission — and you have to be able to show why.

What it's taught me

  • Requirements come before parts.The interesting engineering question is rarely "which component" — it's "what does the instrument actually need," and the part follows from that.
  • Documentation is engineering.On a flight project, an undocumented decision may as well not have been made. Writing it down is the deliverable.
  • Lead time is a design constraint.The best part in the world is the wrong choice if it arrives after integration.
  • Working alongside JPLhas shown me what a professional review standard looks like, and how much of spaceflight is the discipline of justifying yourself clearly.
