01 · Requirements

What the instrument needs

L-MAPS is a radiometer designed to fly on the Artemis Lunar Terrain Vehicle and measure the heat flow beneath it as the rover traverses the surface. Heat flow is one of the more direct ways to probe the Moon's interior structure and thermal history — you're measuring the energy still leaking out of a body that's been cooling for billions of years, and inferring what's underneath from the rate.

Doing that from a moving vehicle, in the lunar thermal environment, is what makes the instrument hard. It has to be sensitive enough to resolve a small signal and rugged enough to survive a launch, a landing, and the surface itself — every RF front-end part has to be justified against that environment, not just against a datasheet.

02 · Design process

Evaluating the front end

Flight hardware moves slowly for a reason. Most of the work is making sure a decision is defensible before it's made.

RF hardware

Parts research

Working through RF component options against the instrument's requirements — what's available, what performs, and what the tradeoffs actually cost us.

Heritage

Flight heritage checks

Verifying that candidate parts have a track record in a comparable environment. Heritage is one of the strongest arguments a spaceflight part can have.

Procurement

Vendor coordination

Sourcing, lead times, documentation, and the planning that keeps a hardware build from stalling on a part nobody ordered in time.

I evaluated and quantified roughly 15 RF front-end components — couplers, switches, amplifiers, filters — pulling specs from vendor datasheets and checking each one against JPL design standards, not just against what the instrument needs on paper.

03 · Construction

Building a Hawaiʻi-assembled front end

Alongside the parts evaluation, I work with the team and our JPL collaborators on mission planning and documentation. It's a mix of careful record-keeping and practical engineering judgment: every component has to make sense for this instrument, in this environment, on this mission — and you have to be able to show why. That evaluation work is what let the team assemble the front end here in Hawaiʻi while still holding to flight-like performance, instead of needing to source a pre-integrated unit from JPL directly.

04 · How I fixed it

Catching problems before they're expensive

On a flight project, the expensive mistakes are the ones that show up after a part is already ordered or already integrated. The heritage check is the mechanism that catches those early — verifying a candidate part has a track record in a comparable environment before it's designed in, not after. Lead time works the same way: the best part in the world is the wrong choice if it arrives after integration, so vendor coordination and lead-time risk are part of the same decision as picking the part itself, not an afterthought.

Documentation is what makes any of that defensible later. On a flight project, an undocumented decision may as well not have been made — writing it down is the deliverable, because it's what a reviewer, or a teammate six months later, actually checks.

05 · Result

What success looked like

Evaluating those ~15 front-end components against JPL's design standards is what enabled a Hawaiʻi-assembled front end with flight-like performance — the instrument didn't have to compromise on performance just because it was being built locally instead of at JPL. Working alongside JPL has also shown me what a professional review standard actually looks like, and how much of spaceflight engineering is the discipline of justifying yourself clearly.
