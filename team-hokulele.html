01 · Requirements

Why flight computers and ground stations matter

A high-power rocket goes places you can't watch it go. Past a few hundred feet, the only way to know altitude, velocity, and where it's going to land is telemetry — a flight computer on board doing the sensing, and a ground station on the ground doing the listening. If either half fails, you don't get a second flight; you get a rocket you have to walk out into the desert to search for.

That's the part of the project I own: making sure the onboard computer is recording and transmitting correctly before we ever light the motor, and making sure the ground station can actually hear it once it's a mile downrange and climbing.

02 · Design process

Choosing the flight computers

Our onboard electronics span AltusMetrum's Fluctus, TeleMega, and TeleBT boards — dual-deploy altimeters with a telemetry link back to the ground.

Onboard

Fluctus, TeleMega & TeleBT

Each stage carries a flight computer: it senses altitude and acceleration, fires the deployment charges at apogee and at a set altitude on the way down, and streams that data out over its radio link the whole flight.

Ground

Yagi antenna

A directional Yagi trades a wide field of view for gain in the direction it's pointed — the right tradeoff when you know roughly where the rocket is going and need range over coverage.

03 · Construction

Building and testing both halves

On the ground, we track the rocket with a receiver on the Yagi antenna. Getting that link right means testing it well before launch day: walking the receiver hardware around, checking reception at range, and making sure the tracking app is reporting altitude, GPS lock, and battery state the way it should before we ever trust it with a live flight.

Field checkout

Range and reception testing

Testing the ground receiver away from the bench — different distances, different orientations — to know its real range before it has to work at the field.

Live tracking

Reading the downlink

The ground app decodes GPS, altitude, velocity, and link health in real time, which is how we know the rocket is still transmitting and where to point the recovery team.

04 · How I fixed it

Catching bad boards in the shop, not on the pad

Bench performance told us very little about what the link would actually do across the field — a link that looks perfect on a desk still needs to be walked out and proven at range. So before every flight, I bench-test each board: confirming the sensors read cleanly, the deployment logic fires at the right points, and the telemetry link is solid, so a bad board gets caught in the shop, not on the pad.

Telemetry is a chain, not a component — the flight computer, the antenna, the receiver, and the software all have to work together, so testing them one at a time isn't the same as testing the link. Recovery depends on data you never actually see used: the whole point of the ground station is telemetry you hope you don't desperately need, until the one flight where you do.

05 · Result

What success looked like

Green Origin is a two-stage rocket, built and refined against last year's design using a more deliberate systems-engineering process the second time around. We shipped it to California on May 1st, and flew it at the FAR-Unlimited competition on May 22–24, 2026 — with the flight computers and ground station both validated and holding up through the flight.
