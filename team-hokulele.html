Why flight computers and ground stations matter

A high-power rocket goes places you can't watch it go. Past a few hundred feet, the only way to know altitude, velocity, and where it's going to land is telemetry — a flight computer on board doing the sensing, and a ground station on the ground doing the listening. If either half fails, you don't get a second flight; you get a rocket you have to walk out into the desert to search for.

That's the part of the project I own: making sure the onboard computer is recording and transmitting correctly before we ever light the motor, and making sure the ground station can actually hear it once it's a mile downrange and climbing.

Testing the flight computers

Our onboard electronics are AltusMetrum TeleBT units — dual-deploy altimeters with a Bluetooth telemetry link.

Onboard

TeleBT flight computer

Each stage carries a TeleBT board: it senses altitude and acceleration, fires the deployment charges at apogee and at a set altitude on the way down, and streams that data out over its radio link the whole flight.

Bench work

Pre-flight checkout

Before a flight, I bench-test each unit — confirming the sensors read cleanly, the deployment logic fires at the right points, and the telemetry link is solid, so a bad board gets caught in the shop, not on the pad.

The ground station

Yagi antennas, aimed and tuned so we don't lose the link the moment the rocket leaves the pad.

On the ground, we track the rocket with a receiver on a directional Yagi antenna — a design that trades a wide field of view for a lot of gain in the direction it's pointed, which is exactly the tradeoff you want when you know roughly where the rocket is going and need range more than coverage. Getting that link right means testing it well before launch day: walking the receiver hardware around, checking reception at range, and making sure the tracking app is reporting altitude, GPS lock, and battery state the way it should before we ever trust it with a live flight.

Field checkout

Range and reception testing

Testing the ground receiver away from the bench — different distances, different orientations — to know its real range before it has to work at the field.

Live tracking

Reading the downlink

The ground app decodes GPS, altitude, velocity, and link health in real time, which is how we know the rocket is still transmitting and where to point the recovery team.

The rocket, and how the season closed out

Green Origin is a two-stage rocket, built and refined against last year's design using a more deliberate systems-engineering process the second time around. We shipped it to California on May 1st, and flew it at the FAR-Unlimited competition on May 22–24, 2026.

  • Telemetry is a chain, not a component.The flight computer, the antenna, the receiver, and the software all have to work together — testing them one at a time isn't the same as testing the link.
  • Field conditions beat bench conditions, every time.A link that looks perfect on a desk still needs to be walked out and proven at range.
  • Recovery depends on data you never see used.The whole point of the ground station is telemetry you hope you don't desperately need — until the one flight where you do.
