HIGP · Student observatory

Ground-based microwave radiometer

A 1.2 m dish, a satellite TV LNB, a software-defined radio, and a Raspberry Pi — turned into an instrument that can actually measure the temperature of the Sun.

Instrument
1.2 m offset dish
Band
Ku-band, via satellite LNB
Result
Sun measured at ~10,000 K
Status
Tracking mount in progress

Why build one

A radiometer doesn't take pictures — it measures power, and converts that power into a temperature. That makes it a genuinely useful teaching instrument, because there's nowhere to hide: if the calibration is wrong, the number is wrong, and the sky will tell you.

I wanted to build one from commodity parts to prove the whole chain could be done on a student budget, and to leave behind something the department could keep using. Consumer satellite TV hardware is remarkably good radio hardware that happens to be cheap, which is what makes the whole project possible.

The signal chain

Every stage adds noise. The job is knowing exactly how much.

Front end

Dish + LNB

A 1.2 m offset dish collects the power; a satellite low-noise block downconverter amplifies it and shifts it down to an IF the radio can digitise.

Digitiser

Software-defined radio

The SDR samples the IF and hands raw complex samples to the Pi, which puts the whole back end of the instrument in software.

Back end

Raspberry Pi + Python

Capture, integration, calibration, and logging all run on the Pi, so the instrument records continuously without a laptop attached.

Calibration — the part that makes it an instrument

Raw SDR counts are meaningless on their own. To turn them into a temperature you need two known points, so I calibrated against a hot load and the cold sky: an ambient-temperature absorber at a known physical temperature at one end, and a patch of empty sky at the other. Those two points give you the gain and offset of the whole chain, and everything measured afterwards is referenced to them.

With that calibration in place, pointing the dish at the Sun returned a brightness temperature of roughly 10,000 K — in the right regime for the quiet Sun at these frequencies through a beam of this size. Seeing a number come out of a pile of consumer parts that agrees with physics is the single most satisfying moment I've had in a lab.

  • Two-point calibration against a hot absorber and cold sky, repeated across sessions to watch for drift.
  • Integration time traded against how quickly the gain wanders — longer averaging lowers noise right up until drift dominates.
  • Beam and pointing matter as much as the electronics: the measured temperature depends on how much of the beam the Sun actually fills.

The Python pipeline

The software side handles capture from the SDR, integration, the two-point calibration solve, and analysis and plotting of the results. Writing it myself meant I had to understand every step between a raw sample and a temperature — which turned out to be the real education in the project. It also means the pipeline is reproducible: the same recorded data run again gives the same answer.

Where it's going

The instrument currently has to be pointed by hand, which caps how long a measurement can run and makes the Sun a moving target. The next step is a motorized tracking mount so it can follow a source across the sky unattended, and from there a permanent station — a student observatory that runs continuously instead of only when someone is standing next to it.
