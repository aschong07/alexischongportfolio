01 · Requirements

The problem

RASC-AL asks student teams to develop a full mission concept to the standard NASA would actually review — architecture, analysis, and a technical paper defended in front of judges. Ours was a crewed lunar surface habitat, and my part of it was the power architecture and the thermal modeling that goes with it.

Those two are the same problem wearing different hats. On the Moon there's no atmosphere to convect heat into, so every watt you generate you eventually have to radiate away. A power system sized for the habitat's peak load is also a heat source the thermal system has to be built around, and the lunar day/night cycle swings the environment at both ends. Sizing one without the other gets you a design that doesn't close.

02 · Design process

What we modeled

Power

Generation architecture

How a fission-based power system could carry the habitat's load through the lunar night, when solar generation isn't an option for two weeks at a time.

Thermal

Heat rejection

Radiator sizing and thermal limits — the constraint that quietly sets the upper bound on how much power the habitat can actually use.

Systems

Requirements & tradeoffs

Balancing mission requirements against mass, complexity, and the engineering realities of building something that has to work far from help.

Subsystems don't have separate answers here — power and thermal had to be sized together with heatmap simulations guiding the fission-based architecture, or neither number meant anything on its own.

04 · How I fixed it

Defending the assumptions, not just the math

The concept finished as a top-14 finalist nationally, which meant defending the work in front of a panel that knew considerably more about it than we did. That review is where the actual fixing happened: reviewers spent far more time on why we assumed something than on the arithmetic that followed, so every weak assumption got surfaced and had to be re-argued or re-modeled on the spot.

Having to justify a modeling assumption out loud to someone who has actually flown hardware is a very fast way to find out which of your numbers you truly believe — and a concept that can't be explained clearly to a room doesn't get evaluated on its merits, no matter how sound the model underneath it is.

05 · Result

What success looked like

Project PETAL finished as a top-14 finalist out of 150+ teams nationally, and earned coverage from Hawaiʻi News Now. The bigger result was learning what a concept actually has to survive to be taken seriously: a model that's only defensible on paper isn't defensible at all.
